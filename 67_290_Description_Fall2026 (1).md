## Why Visualization Matters

We live in a world overflowing with data — but data alone doesn't inform, persuade, or inspire action. It's *visualization* that transforms data into clear, actionable insights that support strategic decisions. This course teaches you to design interactive visualizations that help people see the story inside complex information.

---

## Why This Course Matters Now

Generative AI is rapidly transforming how we work, and data visualization is no exception. The mechanics of producing a chart - or writing the code behind it - have never been easier. This shift does not make this course less relevant; it makes it even more so. When anyone can generate a polished-looking visualization in seconds, the more challenging and valuable work moves upstream: What story are you actually trying to tell? Who is it for, and whose experiences does it represent? What data is available, and just as importantly, what is missing or excluded? How do you present the story so that it is understood, trusted, and acted upon? These are precisely the higher-order questions at the heart of this course - the questions AI cannot answer for you.

---

## Who This Course Is For

You don't need a computer science background to thrive here. Every year this course brings together students from information systems, business, architecture, fine arts, public policy, and beyond, and that mix is a strength, not a hurdle. If you've never written a line of code, you will learn what you need step by step, starting from the fundamentals. If you are an experienced programmer, you will be challenged to think as carefully about design, perception, and storytelling as you do about syntax.

This course is designed to be accessible regardless of your major or technical background:

- **New to coding?** Lab 0 and Lab 1 walk you through Git, GitHub, Node.js, and JavaScript fundamentals from scratch, no prior programming experience assumed.
- **Coming from design, architecture, or the arts?** You'll find a natural home in the course's emphasis on visual perception, composition, and narrative.Your eye for aesthetics is an asset.
- **Coming from business?** The course connects directly to real organizational decision-making, dashboards, and data storytelling for stakeholders.
- **Already comfortable with code or design tools?** You'll be pushed to deepen your thinking about *why* a visualization works, and *why* a good storytelling become more important, not just *how* to build one.

## Course Description

In an increasingly data-driven world, the ability to make sense of complex information is essential for sound decision-making - in business, government, design, science, and everyday life. This course explores the art and science of designing interactive visualizations that communicate data clearly and make its underlying story easy to grasp.

Through a balance of lectures, hands-on labs, and projects, you will develop a practical understanding of the principles behind information design and visualization, including information architecture, human-computer interaction, user-centered design, and the perceptual and cognitive science of how people interpret visual information. You will apply these principles using industry-standard tools and libraries (JavaScript/Observable, Tableau, ArcGIS) to design compelling and trustworthy visual narratives. 

Along the way, you will also develop fluency in AI-assisted workflows, using AI tools thoughtfully as collaborators in the design and coding process - an essential skill in today's AI-augmented workplace.

A core value of this course is to foster cultural and socioeconomic awareness, promote the responsible and ethical use of data and AI, and empower you to apply your technical skills to address real-world social challenges and create a positive impact.

---

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

1. Describe key visualization techniques and design theory, and apply them to communicate information effectively.
2. Apply information systems and data visualization design principles - including data models, visual perception, and cognition - to design and evaluate visualization systems.
3. Synthesize information from multiple sources, accounting for bias and gaps in the data, to communicate quantitative analysis clearly to a specific audience.
4. Critically direct AI-assisted tools in the visualization design and coding process and use them to accelerate execution while applying your own judgment to the questions AI can't answer: e.g., what story to tell, for whom, and why.
6. Evaluate and present the ethical and social implications of data and visualization choices to support positive societal impact.

## AI in This Course

AI-assisted coding and design tools are now part of how visualization professionals work, so they're part of how you will learn here too. Several labs (including Lab 2) explicitly teach you to pair manual coding with AI-assisted workflows, so you understand both *how the code works* and *how to direct an AI tool effectively and critically*.

**Guiding principle:** you are accountable for everything you submit. When specified, you can use AI tools to accelerate your work, explore alternatives, and debug, but not to replace your own understanding of the data or design decisions. When using AI assistants, your responsibilities as a student remain the same. You must follow the academic integrity guidelines of the university and of this class. If you use one of these GenAI tools to develop content for an assignment, you are required to cite the tool’s contribution to your work. If you use GenAI to generate code, then you need to document your work (e.g., prompt used and code generated) in your submission.

- Where a lab or project explicitly builds AI-assisted workflows, using AI tools is expected and part of the graded exercise.
- Where an assignment asks for your own analysis, coding, critique, or design reasoning (e.g., Project 1's visualization critique), your interpretation and voice should be your own. AI can help you explore ideas, but the coding, judgment and taste must be yours. 
- Always be prepared to explain and defend any design or code decision in your submission, regardless of what tool helped you get there.
- When in doubt about what's appropriate for a given assignment, ask before submitting.
