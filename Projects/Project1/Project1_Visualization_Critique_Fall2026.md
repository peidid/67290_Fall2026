# Project 1: Learning to See — A Visualization Critique and Develop Your Own Taste
---

Painters learn to paint by first studying the masters and the failures. That comparative act which is really looking, and asking *why does this work*, or *why doesn't it* is how a painter develops an eye. It cannot be shortcut.

Visualization is no different, and the stakes for developing that eye or taste have never been higher. When anyone or AI can generate a polished-looking visualization instantly, the valuable work moves upstream to the questions that AI cannot answer for you, for example, whether it's honest, whose experience it represents, or whether it tells the story it claims to. That judgment and that taste are something only you can build, and you build it the way painters do: by looking closely, again and again, at other people's work.

This project is your first and most direct exercise in building that taste.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this assignment, you will be able to:

1. Identify what separates strong information visualization from weak, and articulate why in your own words.
2. Critically analyze a visualization for whether it succeeds in telling the story it appears to intend, not just whether it "looks good."
3. Understand how visualizations represent (or fail to represent) cultural and socioeconomic issues, and the people affected by them.
4. Apply best practices, i.e., clarity, accuracy, efficiency, context, and ethics and trust, in evaluating visualization design.
5. Begin developing independent critical judgment about visualization quality, a skill that becomes more valuable, not less, as AI makes production effortless.

## Assignment Tasks

### 1. Select a Social Issue

- Choose a social issue that interests you: They can be health, education, inequality, climate, environment, crime, housing, etc.
- Be specific: If you're interested in health, narrow it: e.g., access to vaccines, or vaccination rates by community.

### 2. Find Visualization Examples

- Select at least two visualizations related to your chosen issue, drawn from reports, research papers, websites, news articles, or other sources.
- Choose a range in terms of quality: for instance, one you consider strong and effective, and one you consider weak, misleading, or in need of improvement. The contrast is where the learning happens.

### 3. Describe Your Chosen Issue and Your Visualizations

Provide brief context for your chosen social issue. For each visualization, briefly describe what is being represented and what the variables are - enough for a reader to understand what they're looking at before you critique it.

### 4. Critique the Visualizations

Before anything else, ask the central question: *what story is this visualization trying to tell, and does it succeed?* A visualization can be technically well-made and still fail this test, or be visually rough and still get it right. Ground your critique in these questions:

- **The story:** What story or argument is this visualization trying to make? Is it explicit (a title, caption) or does it have to be inferred from the design?
- **Whose story:** Who is the audience? Whose experience or data is represented? and whose is missing, minimized, or flattened?
- **Clarity:** Is the visualization easy to understand? Are labels, legends, and axes clearly defined?
- **Accuracy:** Does the visualization present the data in a way that suggests bias, distortion, or a misleading impression?
- **Efficiency:** How effective is the design, e.g., color usage, layout, chart type? Pay attention to non-arbitrary features (see note below).
- **Context:** Does the visualization give the viewer enough context to understand the issue? Are annotations or explanations provided where needed?
- **Ethics and trust:** Does the visualization represent the people and communities behind the data honestly and respectfully? Does it earn the viewer's trust through integrity, rather than through manipulation, sensationalism, or oversimplification?

For each visualization: if you think it's effective, explain specifically what makes it work. If you think it's weak, explain what makes it hard to understand or trust, and how it could be improved.

### 5. AI Reflection

Use a generative AI tool (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Copilot) to critique one of your selected visualizations. Include:

- **AI Prompt:** the prompt you used.
- **Comparison:** briefly compare the AI's critique with your own. What did the AI do well? What did it miss or get wrong?
- **Reflection:** one benefit and one limitation of using AI to evaluate data visualizations.

*Use AI as a supporting tool, not a replacement for your own analysis. The questions in Task 4 - what story, for whom, what's missing - are exactly the ones AI cannot answer for you; your job is to show that judgment yourself first.*

### 6. Present in Class

Present your topic, your visualization selection, and your critique to the class.

### A Note on Non-Arbitrary Features

Non-arbitrary features are design choices that are deliberate and directly serve the visualization's purpose; they are not random or decorative. For example: *Color coding* which uses different colors to represent categories or highlight a trend, making the data easier to interpret; and *data grouping or sorting* which orders data (e.g., ascending/descending) to deliberately reveal a pattern or insight.
